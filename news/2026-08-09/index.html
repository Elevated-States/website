The week Big Pharma stopped hedging

This week in psychedelic science, policy & culture
Week of August 3, 2026

This was the week Big Pharma stopped hedging. Eli Lilly wrote a check that could reach $3.8 billion, the FDA quietly published the rulebook that will decide whether any of these drugs ever reach a pharmacy, and a study in a top psychiatry journal reminded everyone why that rulebook is so hard to write. Below: what actually happened, and — more usefully — the several ways smart people are reading each of it. We try to give you the argument, not just the headline.

The Big Stories

Business

Eli Lilly bets up to $3.8 billion on psychedelics — the biggest deal the field has ever seen

On July 16, Eli Lilly announced it would acquire the psychedelics biotech AtaiBeckley for $2.8 billion up front, with another $1 billion tied to milestones. The prize is BPL-003, a fast-acting, single-dose nasal spray derived from 5-MeO-DMT (a close cousin of DMT), now in late-stage trials for treatment-resistant depression. It is the largest acquisition in the sector's history — and it comes less than a year after AbbVie paid up to $1.2 billion for a different psychedelic candidate. One founder-investor, Christian Angermayer, reportedly cleared around $360 million personally.

How it's being read

Validation, finally. To bulls, this is the moment psychedelics graduated from fringe to pipeline. Big Pharma, flush with GLP-1 (weight-loss drug) money, is now willing to make a serious neuroscience bet — and Lilly's due diligence is its own stamp of credibility.

Or "pharma capture." Skeptics point out which molecule Lilly bought: a short-acting, patentable, in-clinic drug that leans away from the long, therapy-heavy classic-psychedelic model the movement was built on. The worry is that the version of this medicine that survives is the one that looks most like a normal pharmaceutical — and least like a transformative experience.

The sober middle. Trial results aren't expected until around 2029. A big check is a bet, not an approval — and depression drugs have humbled deeper-pocketed buyers before.

Science / FDA

The FDA finalized its psychedelic rulebook — and the whole game now turns on one hard problem

On July 14, the FDA published its final guidance, "Psychedelic Drugs: Considerations for Clinical Investigations," completing a draft first floated in 2023. Most of it circles a single issue: functional unblinding — the fact that people almost always know whether they got the real drug or a placebo, because one of them is obviously, profoundly psychedelic. That problem helped sink the MDMA-therapy application in 2024. The FDA's answer includes blinding-integrity questionnaires and a clever workaround: pairing a placebo study with a separate dose-response study (low vs. high dose, no placebo at all).

How it's being read

A receptive regulator. Industry analysts read the guidance as the FDA building the on-ramp — giving sponsors a real, if narrow, path through a genuinely thorny methodological problem.

A study-design reckoning. Methodologists call it overdue honesty: the agency is effectively admitting that the gold-standard placebo trial doesn't work cleanly for a drug you can't hide. Better to design around it than pretend.

A higher, costlier bar. Critics note the guidance is non-binding and adds expense — two trained monitors per session, extra study arms — which small companies and nonprofits can least afford. And "clinically meaningful" is still left undefined, so the goalposts remain a little blurry.

Science

Compass's psilocybin cleared its second big trial — and the debate is now about magnitude, not whether it "works"

On July 7, Compass Pathways reported 26-week data from COMP006, its second Phase 3 trial of synthetic psilocybin (COMP360) for treatment-resistant depression. About 39% of patients on the 25 mg dose hit a clinically meaningful response by week 6 after two doses, and the effect largely held for six months — better than the single-dose result in its first trial. The company plans to file for FDA approval in the fourth quarter of 2026, which would tee up a possible decision on the first-ever approved classic psychedelic in early 2027.

How it's being read

Durability is the story. Supporters emphasize that a single course of treatment holding for six months is genuinely unusual in this hardest-to-treat group, with a clean safety profile and no suicidality red flags.

"Cautious optimism, lingering questions." Other researchers note the effect size is statistically real but modest, and the response bar Compass used isn't the field's standard — so the honest question is whether the benefit clearly beats existing options.

The unblinding asterisk. And because psilocybin can't be hidden from patients (see above), some argue the true drug-specific effect could be smaller still once expectation is accounted for.

Science

The uncomfortable paper: blinding fails in more than 90% of psychedelic trials

On July 1, JAMA Psychiatry published a systematic review quantifying just how leaky these trials are. Across psilocybin, LSD, and ayahuasca studies, both patients and even the raters scoring them correctly guessed who got the real drug more than 90% of the time. This isn't a fringe critique anymore — it's the central methodological fault line of the entire field, and it's why the FDA guidance above exists.

How it's being read

The deflating case. If nearly everyone knows what they took, then expectation and enthusiasm are baked into the results. One meta-analysis cited alongside the review suggests that, adjusted for equal unblinding, psychedelic therapy might perform no better than conventional antidepressants.

The reform case. Many researchers say the fix isn't to give up but to change the yardstick — treat these as effectively open-label treatments and measure them honestly against open-label standard care.

The defenders. Others counter that durable, months-long remission and clear biological effects are hard to explain by hope alone — expectation doesn't usually last six months.

The Policy Desk

Veterans

The VA and HHS formally teamed up on psychedelic therapy for veterans

In mid-July, the Department of Veterans Affairs and Health and Human Services signed agreements to coordinate psychedelic research — expanding trials, training clinicians to administer these drugs if they're ever approved, and pooling data for the FDA. The VA is now involved in 20-plus psychedelic trials, building on an April executive order that committed at least $50 million, much of it toward ibogaine for addiction and traumatic brain injury.

How it's being read

Overdue access. Veteran advocates cheer it — many currently fly to Mexico or Israel and pay out of pocket, and PTSD and veteran suicide remain the emotional and political engine of the whole reform movement.

Mind the gap between headline and reality. The VA itself is careful to note these are still Schedule I drugs, not approved care, and discouraged veterans from self-medicating. A RAND study this year found veterans' support for legal MDMA is no higher than the general public's — the enthusiasm may be narrower than the coverage suggests.

Congress & the States

Washington nibbles, the states leap: ibogaine, a defense-bill rider, and Oregon's squeeze

Two things are true at once. Federally, progress is incremental: the House attached psychedelic-research provisions to the annual defense bill, and a bipartisan group introduced the IBOGAINE Act to force a review of moving ibogaine off Schedule I — but both still have to survive the Senate, and a review isn't a reschedule. The real action is in the states. Colorado's ibogaine research law takes effect August 12. Texas is pressing ahead with up to $50 million for ibogaine trials — the largest state investment in psychedelic medicine ever — with Mississippi and West Virginia signaling they'll join. And Oregon moved to fold its pioneering psilocybin program into its cannabis bureaucracy while proposing to roughly double some fees.

How it's being read

Federalism as accelerator. With Congress slow, red and blue states alike are running the experiment themselves — and a Texas-led, veteran-framed ibogaine push has bipartisan momentum that D.C. lacks.

Cracks in the model. Oregon's fee hikes land on an already-struggling licensee base, a warning that "legalize it" is the easy part and "make the economics work" is the hard one. And ibogaine's real cardiac risks mean even supporters frame these as tightly supervised research programs, not open access.

Also Worth Knowing

Harm reduction gets an upgrade

Drug-checking is professionalizing: a new operator took over DanceSafe's test-kit production and distribution this year, and a next-gen portable "Pen Test" checking device launched in June. Advocates still can't get on-site checking into many of the largest U.S. festivals — the same "aren't you condoning use?" friction the whole harm-reduction field keeps running into.

Record money, concentrated

Second-quarter psychedelic financing topped roughly $1 billion — but the bulk came from a single ~$805M raise by an LSD developer. Capital is flooding in and pooling around a few large, pharma-friendly bets rather than spreading across access and therapy models.

Ketamine's reckoning

The telehealth ketamine boom is meeting its regulatory moment: the DEA extended remote-prescribing flexibility through year's end, Georgia began regulating ketamine clinics as "psychedelic-assisted treatment," and wrongful-death suits keep mounting — the after-echo of the Matthew Perry case still hanging over the industry.

A quiet tension: peyote and reciprocity

As mescaline-decriminalization proposals spread, many Native American Church leaders are pushing back — a scarce, slow-growing sacred plant already strained by overharvest doesn't need new demand. A reminder that "more access" and "protect the source" don't always point the same way.

The Elevated View

Zoom out and the week tells one story: psychedelics are being medicalized and monetized faster than they're being understood. A $3.8 billion deal and a fast-tracked approval on one side; a top journal saying we can't even properly blind the trials on the other. Both are real. Both are this week.

That gap — between the hype and the honest science — is exactly the space worth standing in. Not the booster who oversells, not the cynic who dismisses, but the person willing to hold the excitement and the asterisk at the same time. That's the whole point of paying attention every week.
